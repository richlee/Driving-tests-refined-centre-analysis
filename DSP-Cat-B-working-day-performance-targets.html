1. Recommended starting point

Monthly volumes support a useful working-day workload estimate. They do not reveal the busiest individual day or the size of intraday bursts. The observed variation between monthly working-day averages is comfortably smaller than 50%, but that does not establish that 50% will cover both future growth and every daily peak.

Busy current-day baseline8,500completed-test-equivalent journeys
Provisional growth workload13,000busy baseline plus 50%, rounded up
Additional resilience workload16,000growth plus an assumed daily surge

Use these as candidate business-volume inputs for a representative working-day model. They are not HTTP request rates, measured daily maxima, concurrent-user counts or independently agreed pass/fail thresholds. Additional DSP activity must be modelled explicitly.

Recommended decision: adopt 13,000 completed-test-equivalent journeys for a provisional 50%-growth day; exercise 16,000 for a stronger resilience day. Run both with slot-shaped arrivals and completion bursts, rather than a uniform stream. Confirm the workflow mix and service-level objectives before treating either as an acceptance requirement.

The busiest month’s daily equivalent was 13.5% above the overall average in the financial-year window, and 5.0% above it in the latest twelve months. The two windows overlap and are not independent samples. Their difference is not proof that underlying daily volatility has fallen.

Working-day equivalent, not observed weekday traffic. The numerator includes tests completed at weekends and on bank holidays. Allocating all of them to ordinary working days produces a higher average than using only weekday completions with the same denominator, but it does not establish an upper bound for any individual weekday. No daily values are observed or reconstructed.

Calendar normalisation matters. For example, May 2026 contains 160,508 England/Wales completions and 19 ordinary working days, giving approximately 8,448 per working day. March 2026 has more completions (183,381), but 22 working days, giving approximately 8,336 per day. The largest raw monthly total need not imply the largest daily equivalent.

3. Which standard deviation is defensible?

Calculate variation across all twelve month-level rates, weighted by working days. The maximum and minimum alone cannot determine a standard deviation. For month m, let Nm be completed tests, Wm ordinary working days, and rm the working-day-equivalent rate.

Monthly rate: rm = Nm / Wm

Weighted mean: μ = ΣNm / ΣWm

Between-month variance: σ² = Σ[Wm × (rm − μ)²] / ΣWm

Standard deviation: σ = √σ²

Coefficient of variation: CV = σ / μ

This is a descriptive, weighted population standard deviation of the selected historical monthly rates. There is no sample-size correction: the purpose is to describe those twelve months, not infer a population variance from a random sample of months. It includes changes in capacity, trend and seasonality; it is not an isolated seasonal effect.

The latest result is approximately 373 tests per working-day equivalent, or 4.6%. The earlier financial-year result is approximately 581, or 7.9%. These are modest relative to a 50% business-volume allowance, but neither is the standard deviation of actual daily traffic.

Why daily variation remains unknown

A hypothetical month with 160,000 tests spread over 20 working days could contain 8,000 every day, giving daily SD zero. The same monthly total could instead contain ten days at 6,000 and ten at 10,000, giving daily population SD 2,000. Both have the same monthly average. This is a mathematical illustration, not an estimate of DVSA’s daily distribution.

Therefore the monthly evidence cannot justify a daily 95th/99th percentile, a normal distribution, or a claim that the test target covers “three standard deviations” of actual days. It also cannot support a Poisson assumption simply because the data are counts: scheduled tests and synchronised working patterns are not independent random arrivals.

“Significant” here is an engineering judgement about the load envelope and the service objectives, not a statistical significance test. Confidence is high in the historical arithmetic and low in any unmeasured daily-tail claim.

Adding 50% to the latest overall average gives about 12,063 tests. Adding it to the busiest monthly rate gives about 12,672. Rounding the latter upwards yields a 13,000-journey growth workload.

Growth and surge allowances multiply. If 50% is intended as future business growth, it is already consumed by that growth. A further peak allowance must be applied separately:

8,500 × 1.50 × 1.20 = 15,300

Rounded resilience workload: 16,000 completed-test-equivalent journeys

The 20% surge is an explicit design assumption, not a measured daily percentile. Using 16,000 provides approximately 25.5% more volume than the unrounded 12,750 growth baseline. It is a useful stress scenario; monthly totals cannot establish its probability or guarantee it covers every real day.

5. Turning daily volumes into a meaningful DSP test

Preserve the shape of a working day. A daily total controls how much business work is simulated. The timing, concurrent users, journey mix and processing cost determine how hard that work is for DSP.

Daily volume is not a request rate

If an illustrative day lasts eight hours, 13,000 tests spread uniformly across it correspond to only 0.45 test journeys per second. That is not 0.45 HTTP requests per second: each journey may cause many requests, writes and downstream operations. Uniform arrival rates can conceal short-lived bottlenecks.

The agreed workload model should map a test journey to the actual DSP actions in scope. Depending on the implemented service, these may include obtaining the day’s work, accessing an appointment, recording progress, submitting an outcome and triggering downstream processing. These are modelling prompts, not verified statements about DSP’s current interfaces.

A separate concurrency and burst scenario

The previously discussed 1,500 England/Wales examiner FTE is a planning estimate, not an official centre staffing extract. Scaling it by 50% gives 2,250 FTE. Using 2,250 active examiner sessions is therefore a candidate concurrency scenario, but must be validated against actual headcount, part-time patterns, deployments and simultaneous activity.

Illustrative synchronised completion burst:

2,250 result-submission journeys / 60 seconds = 37.5 journeys per second

If each journey generates k calls in that interval, its average contribution is 37.5 × k calls per second, before other traffic.

This is an assumed burst, not observed telemetry. Specify the request-arrival pattern within the minute as well as its average. Do not make each of 2,250 sessions generate traffic continuously merely because the session is open. Daily volume and concurrency are separate controls: the 16,000-journey stress workload is not inferred from 2,250 FTE, and may deliberately exceed a simple seven-tests-per-FTE illustration.

For growth acceptance, demonstrate that the 13,000-journey working day meets the agreed service objectives under the agreed peaks. Use the 16,000-journey case to assess resilience and recovery, with explicit expected behaviour. A short burst run does not replace a full-day endurance run.

Evidence that would tighten the targets

Prioritise daily scheduled and completed test counts by centre; active examiner headcount by day; timestamped client/API activity; the mix of non-completed appointments; and submission/retry patterns. These would allow actual weekday effects, daily percentiles and intraday peaks to replace the current assumptions. More monthly averages alone will not resolve those gaps.
